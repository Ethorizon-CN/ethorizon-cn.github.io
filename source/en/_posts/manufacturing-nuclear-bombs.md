### Getting to Work

Once everything is ready, the first thing to do is split the uranium-235 you've gathered in batches into two equal halves and put them in a pair of hemispherical containers, which you might make using an acetylene torch (Acetylene Torch). Uranium melts at 414.2°C, while an acetylene torch burns at 526.4°C, so in theory an acetylene torch is hot enough to melt uranium-235. Perhaps you should build a kiln out of a few dozen firebricks and add a bellows, which would work better; but if you're patient and have some luck on your side (since uranium can explode when it burns), an acetylene torch should be good enough. Once the uranium melts into liquid and flows into a hemispherical mold (refractory clay for ceramics will come in handy here), then once the first hemisphere has been made and cooled, move it aside and make the second.

When the uranium is melting, staying 50 feet ~~(about 15.24 m)~~ away and shielding yourself with 5 tons of lead should be safe enough. Once the work of dividing the uranium-235 into two piles is done, you should pack them separately in lead boxes. Then dig a hole from the second floor through to the basement, and connect the two with a pair of black iron pipes, making the total length about 20 feet ~~(about 6.10 m)~~ or so. It might be slightly better if you can coat the outside of the pipe with 6 inches ~~(about 15.24 cm)~~ of cement, but if the floor is solid enough and the house is built on rock, you can skip that hassle too. Before lowering the pipe, first place the hemispherical container holding the uranium flat side up on the concrete, then position the pipe properly, and the atomic bomb is already half finished.

To keep the uranium from escaping, the basement should be filled with a mixture of sand, gravel, cement, and water, but since this only needs to work once, it doesn't matter whether it looks good. What really matters is that there is enough blocking force outside the pipe so that the uranium doesn't leak out before the bomb explodes. Actually, a semi-liquid sand-and-mud mixture is enough to do the job. And just like that, the receiving part of the atomic bomb is finished. The detonating part is harder to make; one idea is to place the other hemispherical container at the upper end of the pipe so that, when detonated, it topples down into the receiving part below.

In principle it's simple, but some technical difficulties are not easy to solve. For instance, if the hemispherical container used for detonation is placed even slightly crooked, the uranium will slide down along the pipe, and then your dream of becoming a terrorist comes to nothing, because that kind of death won't make anyone feel terror — it'll only become a joke for after-dinner conversation ~~666~~. To make the joint stronger, you can drill a few holes in this part and drive iron nails into them. Then take a 3-foot ~~(about 7.62 cm)~~ length of 2.5-inch ~~(about 6.35 cm)~~ copper pipe, fill it with molten lead, and set the detonating hemispherical container into a matching groove in the lead base; then drive another iron rod about a foot into the other end of the pipe. The total weight of this device is 80\~95 pounds ~~(about 36.29\~43.09 kg)~~. Finally, screw a threaded cap onto the top of the pipe; when it can be turned freely and tightly, take it off again, drill a hole in it big enough to accommodate the detonating device rod, and leave 6\~8 inches ~~(about 15.24\~20.32 cm)~~ of the rod exposed above the hole, with exactly one nail hole drilled in the rod.

Try fitting nails of various sizes; the most suitable size is one that fits the whole detonating device (of course, when test-fitting, don't do it at the upper end of the receiving pipe, to avoid danger). Then spread TNT or explosive onto a dish (preferably the base of a coffee pot filter), stuff it in, and insert one or two detonators. ### Radiation Protection Equipment

In order to explain all the important steps clearly, a few small issues should be clarified as well.

For example, as briefly mentioned earlier, when using an acetylene torch you have to consider the possibility that the uranium may catch fire and burn. Actually, it should be said that the entire mechanical operation should be carried out in an "emulsion tank".

For those not very familiar with mechanical technical operations, an emulsion is a liquid that looks like milk and shares many similarities with oil, but will not catch fire and burn.

This kind of emulsion is easy to buy from ordinary machine shop suppliers, and no one will ask you what you're buying it for. Using this emulsion can reduce the danger to a minimum.

In fact, if we want to dissolve uranium or perform mechanical processing on it, it's safest to do so in a pure nitrogen atmosphere. But if you're careful enough and lucky enough, then there's no need to use such an extreme safety method.

The radiation problem is the more troublesome point. Radium's radiation is proportional to its weight, but uranium's radiation is exponentially related to its weight (this property is also what makes it explosive). Since the mass in each hemisphere exceeds half the critical value, being in the same room with them is extremely dangerous. Breathing in even a little air containing radioactive dust means you're about to leave this world.

Therefore I suggest that all workers should have a separate oxygen supply; putting an oxygen tube in each person's mouth could solve this problem.

But solving the radiation problem comprehensively may be more troublesome; still, with determination plus wisdom and luck, this problem can be overcome. I would also suggest using a powered wheelchair with a lead shell, so the operator can sit inside and work safely. Just open a small slit in it, use leaded glass as a window, and the operator can see outside. Lead sleeves and gloves can be used for mechanical actions that require contact with uranium.

To prevent radiation from escaping, the walls and floor of the entire house need to be covered with a layer of lead; a layer of lead sheeting must also be added to the basement ceiling, to avoid radiation problems from the receiving part. By my count, at least 6~8 tons of lead would be needed to maintain basic safety. That in turn means more work to reinforce the floor so it doesn't collapse. Once all that work is done, you can start building the atomic bomb. If you want to make a "braised rabbit" dinner, the first step in opening the cookbook is to catch a rabbit! ### Separation Techniques

Next we need to consider what method to use to separate uranium-235.

For terrorists, the gaseous diffusion method is one of the good options; this was also used in the early days of making atomic bombs, and it is reliable and does not require overly complex technology. However, it costs more, and the chemicals used are even more frightening. First, you need about 12 miles ~~(about 19.31 km)~~ of special glass-lined steel pipe, and use 60 tons of hydrofluoric acid ( $HF$ ) to form uranium hexafluoride, then blow it against a membrane with special small pores. Because uranium hexafluoride-238 is heavier, it gets trapped when passing through this membrane and does not pass through easily.

Each pass can increase the uranium-235 content by 0.5%; by repeating this procedure, the resulting uranium hexafluoride-235 content becomes greater and greater, and in the end you only need to separate the fluorine out of the uranium hexafluoride. Because hydrofluoric acid is very expensive and hard to obtain, it's best to steal some, or else just go steal a few million US dollars first. ~~How is this author so experienced~~

If you think this path is a dead end, there are other methods. You can build a breeder reactor (Breeder Reactor) in the woods, use uranium to make plutonium, and then separate it using chemical techniques. As for how to build a breeder reactor, that's not hard either — any university textbook can tell you several methods. Although there is no theoretical difficulty, there are practical difficulties. But if you happen to have your own private stream, several trainloads of sodium, a considerable amount of stainless steel pipe, and a hundred acres of land isolated from the outside world, then there's no problem.  ~~then that's easy to do~~

If you're not interested in either of these two methods, there are some interesting new techniques to consider. You can first use a cryogenic magnet (Cryogenic Magnet), which at liquid helium temperature (about -270°C) can maintain a magnetic field of 20,000 gauss... but, um, the procedure after that is too complicated...

There is also a method using lasers, because uranium-238 is heavier, so after being irradiated by the laser beam its motion deviates at a smaller angle than uranium-235. So if you spread a layer of uranium on a plane perpendicular to the laser light, uranium-235 and uranium-238 can be separated by their deflection angles. In principle this method is simple and feasible, but it is far too slow in terms of time. In a day it can process only about 20 pounds ~~(about 9.07 kg)~~ of uranium (containing 235 and 238), and the separation efficiency is about 12.5%; each pass can produce about 10% uranium-235, so you'd have to process it 9 times to reach atomic bomb standard.

By that calculation, separating 36 pounds ~~(about 16.33 kg)~~ of 97% purity uranium-235 from 9,600 pounds ~~(about 4.35 t)~~ of fuel rods would take about 4 years. However, the radiation from it means you simply don't have 4 years left to live, so you'll have to find two or three volunteers to fulfill your unfinished ambition.
